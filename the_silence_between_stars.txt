The universe is, above all, a place of extraordinary emptiness. Between each star lies an
unimaginable void — distances so vast that even light, the fastest thing we know, takes years
to cross them. Yet this emptiness is not nothing. It is threaded with magnetic fields, drifting
clouds of hydrogen, the faint echoes of ancient explosions. Space is silent not because it is
dead, but because sound has no medium to travel through it.

Humanity has always looked upward and felt something difficult to name. It is not quite
longing, not quite fear — perhaps it is the quiet recognition of our own smallness against
something incomprehensibly large. The stars we see at night are not a ceiling but a depth, a
tunnel of time stretching billions of years into the past. When we observe a distant galaxy,
we are not seeing it as it is, but as it was — a fossil of light, preserved across the cosmos,
And yet, from within this vastness, a small planet grew oceans, then life, then curiosity. The
same atoms forged in stellar cores now sit inside human skulls, wondering about their origins.

There is something almost paradoxical about it: the universe produced minds capable of
comprehending, even partially, the universe itself. Whether that means anything is a question
science cannot answer — but it remains, perhaps, the most beautiful question we have ever
learned to ask.
